    Faults under the map

    The blue lines are active fault traces from the GEM Global Active Faults Database. Switch them on and off in the map layers.

    A fault is a fracture where blocks of rock move past each other. In the Himalaya, the Indian plate pushes under Tibet, and much of that push is taken up by thrust faults along the southern foothills. Tibet itself is also pulled apart and sheared by other faults.

    Not every earthquake sits on a mapped line. Some large earthquakes begin on faults that never reach the surface, and mapped traces are simplified. Treat the lines as context, not as a prediction.

    Thrust or reverseRock on one side is pushed up and over the other. The Main Frontal Thrust is an example.
    Strike-slipBlocks slide sideways past each other. The Karakoram Fault is an example. Oblique faults are shown with this pattern.
    NormalThe crust is stretched and one block drops. Many of the north-south rifts on the Tibetan Plateau are of this type.

    When the ground moves

    You cannot predict an earthquake, but you can prepare for one. These steps follow the guidance published by national disaster agencies.

    Step 1
    Drop

    Get down on your hands and knees so the shaking cannot knock you over.

    Step 2
    Cover

    Protect your head and neck with your arms. Crawl under a sturdy table if one is within a few steps.

    Step 3
    Hold on

    Hold your shelter and move with it until the shaking stops.

    Before

    • Fix tall furniture, shelves and water heaters to the wall.
    • Pack a bag with water, a torch, a first-aid kit, medicines, a power bank and copies of key documents.
    • Agree on a meeting place with your family and learn how to switch off gas and electricity.
    • Pick a safe spot in every room, away from windows, mirrors and heavy objects that can fall.

    During

    • Indoors: stay inside. Do not run for the stairs and do not use a lift.
    • In bed: stay there and cover your head with a pillow.
    • Outdoors: move to open ground, away from buildings, trees and power lines.
    • In a vehicle: stop in a clear place, away from bridges and slopes, and stay inside.
    • On mountain roads and trails: expect falling rock and landslides, and move away from steep slopes.

    After

    • Expect aftershocks. Drop, cover and hold on each time.
    • Check yourself and others for injuries and give first aid.
    • If you smell gas or see damaged wiring, switch off the supply and leave.
    • Send text messages instead of calling, so networks stay free for emergencies.
    • Stay out of damaged buildings and off cracked or loosened slopes.

    In India, dial 112 for police, fire and ambulance. Learn more from the National Disaster Management Authority, Ready.gov and ShakeOut.

    Sources and method

    Every earthquake on this page comes from the USGS catalog. Nothing is estimated, smoothed or invented, and no values are typed in by hand.

    Data sources

    • Earthquakes: USGS Earthquake CatalogQueried through the FDSN Event Web Service for the box 24°N to 37°N and 72°E to 98°E. Only events typed as earthquakes are requested.
    • Faults: GEM Global Active Faults DatabaseStyron, R. and Pagani, M. (2020), Earthquake Spectra 36(1_suppl), doi:10.1177/8755293020944182. Data from the GEM repository under CC BY-SA 4.0. Clipped to the map area, simplified, and fold axes removed.
    • Rivers, lakes, coastline, peaks, cities, ranges: Natural EarthNatural Earth vector data (public domain), version 5.2.0-pre, clipped and simplified.
    • Official hazard informationIndia: National Center for Seismology. Nepal: National Seismological Centre.

    How to read the map

    • Magnitude classesLight (4 to 4.9), Moderate (5 to 5.9), Strong (6 to 6.9), Major (7 to 7.9) and Great (8 and above), as used by USGS. Circle area is not proportional to energy. Size grows with magnitude so large events stand out.
    • Depth classesShallow is under 70 km, intermediate is 70 to 300 km, and deep is over 300 km. Shallow earthquakes usually cause the strongest shaking at the surface.
    • TimeAll times are UTC.

    Limits of this project

    • This is a map of past earthquakes. It cannot predict when or where the next one will happen, and it is not an early-warning system.
    • Early records are less certain. Before seismometer networks grew, small earthquakes went unrecorded, and locations and magnitudes carry larger errors. Counts over time reflect recording as well as seismic activity.
    • Magnitudes mix several types (Mw, mb, Ms) depending on the event and the era.
    • Fault traces are simplified and cover only faults that have been mapped.
    • The map draws no political boundaries. It shows seismic data, not a political map.
    • For official seismic zones and building codes, use the national agencies linked at left.
